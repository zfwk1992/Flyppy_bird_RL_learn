The bird on the right is a neural network. Nobody wrote a rule telling it when to flap — it was trained by reinforcement learning, which means it played the game a few tens of thousands of times and kept whatever happened to work. What follows is how that works in general, then what this particular network looks like, what goes into it, what comes out, and how well it actually plays.

Part 1 · How reinforcement learning works

The ideaNobody tells it the right answer

The vocabulary is small and worth having up front. A state is what it can see right now. A policy is how it turns that into an action. An episode is one life, from start to crash. The return is everything it collected over that life. Training means searching for a policy whose expected return is high — by playing, not by being taught. This one played about 20,000 games and made 2.5 million decisions to get where it is.

ObjectiveThree numbers, and a guess about the future

From that, the network learns Q(s,a): how much total future reward it expects if it takes action a right now. Acting is just picking the bigger of the two numbers. The rest of this page is about where those two numbers come from.

ValueSqueezing the whole future into one number

This number has a name — Q(s,a), the value of taking action a in state s. It is the whole rest of the game added up and compressed into a single figure — with a discount γ along the way, so that a reward arriving k steps from now counts as γk today. And it is the reason the agent needs no plan and no search: if the two numbers are right, then always taking the larger one is optimal play. Finding good values and finding a good policy turn out to be the same problem.

The trickLearning without waiting for the ending

This is the idea the whole field rests on. Waiting until the bird dies to find out whether a flap was good would waste almost every frame. Instead, one step is enough: the reward actually collected, plus what the agent already believes about where it landed. Early on those beliefs are nonsense, so the corrections are nonsense too — but the real rewards, +1 and −1, are not guesses. They leak backwards one step at a time, and slowly drag the whole estimate towards the truth.

Why a networkThe table would not fit in the universe

Classical reinforcement learning kept values in a table, one row per situation. That works for a board with a few thousand positions and collapses here. The network replaces the table with something far smaller that has to generalise: pictures that look alike get similar values, so a situation encountered for the first time still produces a sensible answer. That is the entire meaning of the word deep in deep reinforcement learning — the learning part is older than the network, and the network is only there because the table cannot exist.

How it learnsThe training loop

The target net is just a copy of the online one, frozen for a thousand steps at a time. Without it, the network would be chasing a target it moves itself every step, and chasing a moving target is how learning diverges instead of settling.

One piece is missing from that diagram: at the start the agent isn't choosing at all. ε begins at 1.0 — every action a coin flip — and only decays to 0.01 over the first half-million decisions. Without it the agent would never stumble onto the flap that clears a high gap — you cannot learn that something is a good idea if you never try it. Once trained, the randomness is switched off and it simply takes the better number every time.

Part 2 · This particular network

Architecture1,258,659 parameters

The convolutions look for shapes regardless of where on the screen they appear — a gap edge is a gap edge whether it is high or low. That is why the same handful of layers works for Flappy Bird, for Atari, and for a great many things that are not games at all.

The split at the end is worth a word. Instead of producing the two values directly, the network estimates how good the situation is (V) and how much each action changes that (A), then adds them back together. Most frames are situations where the choice barely matters, and separating the two lets the network learn about the situation from every frame rather than only from the action it happened to take.

Part 3 · What goes in, what comes out

InputFour black-and-white frames, nothing else

A single frame is a still picture: it cannot tell you whether the bird is rising or falling. So the network is handed four consecutive frames at once, and reads the motion out of the differences between them.

shape4 × 80 × 128 — four stacked frames
valuesone bit per pixel: object or background
sourcethe same picture you see, shrunk 3.6× wide and 4× tall
not includedcoordinates, velocity, gap size, distance to the pipe

OutputTwo numbers, thirty times a second

shape2 floats — Q(flap) and Q(don't)
decisiontake the larger; no randomness once trained
rateone decision every 4 frames — 7.5 per second at 30 fps
costabout 12.3 million multiply-adds per decision

Everything the agent does comes out of this one comparison. It runs entirely in your browser — the weights are downloaded once and the network is evaluated on your own device, which is why it keeps playing at the same strength no matter how many people open the page.

The spread is the interesting part. Every pipe is roughly an independent coin flip at about one chance in a hundred of dying, which makes short runs and enormous runs both perfectly normal: a quarter of the games end before pipe 29, and a quarter go past 151. So a single round tells you very little about how good it is — if you want a fair impression, watch it for a while.
